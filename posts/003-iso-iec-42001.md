# 3. ISO/IEC 42001 — AI Management System Standard

*Posted 24 September 2026*

Where the EU AI Act sets binding legal obligations and the NIST AI RMF gives
a voluntary risk-management vocabulary, **ISO/IEC 42001** is the first
international standard you can actually get *certified against* for how you
govern AI. Published in December 2023, it defines requirements for an
"AI Management System" (AIMS) — the organizational processes and controls
around how AI is designed, developed, deployed, and monitored.

## What it actually is

ISO 42001 follows the same "Harmonized Structure" as familiar standards like
ISO 27001 (information security) and ISO 9001 (quality management):

- **Clauses 4–10** define the management system itself — context of the
  organization, leadership commitment, planning and risk treatment,
  support and resources, operational controls, performance evaluation, and
  continual improvement. This is the Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle applied
  to AI governance.
- **Annex A** lists 38 specific controls grouped into nine objectives:
  AI policy, internal organization/roles, resources (including compute and
  data), impact assessment, AI system life cycle management, data
  management, information for interested parties (transparency), responsible
  use, and third-party/supplier relationships.

Importantly, you don't have to implement all 38 controls. Clause 6.1.3 asks
you to determine which controls your own risk assessment calls for, then
cross-check that set against Annex A to make sure nothing relevant was
missed — the same "Statement of Applicability" logic ISO 27001 uses.

## Certification — what it does and doesn't cover

- Certification applies to the **management system**, not to a specific
  model, product, or output — you can be ISO 42001 certified and still ship
  a biased or unsafe model if the surrounding governance process was
  followed on paper.
- It's **voluntary**, but increasingly shows up as a procurement requirement
  — enterprise customers and public-sector buyers use it as a trust signal
  when selecting AI vendors.
- Certification requires a paid audit from an accredited, independent
  certification body (there's no free or self-attested path). Costs
  typically run in the tens of thousands of dollars depending on
  organization size and audit scope.

## Where it sits relative to the EU AI Act

This is the practical question most teams actually have. As of 2026,
ISO/IEC 42001 (adopted in Europe as EN ISO/IEC 42001:2026) is **not** a
harmonized standard under the EU AI Act — meaning certification does not by
itself grant a legal presumption of conformity with the Act's high-risk
requirements. Standards bodies are still working through EU-harmonized,
AI-Act-specific standards separately. In the meantime, ISO 42001 is best
treated as strong supporting evidence of a functioning governance process,
not a substitute for the Act's own conformity assessment obligations.

## What this means in practice

- **Use it to organize what you're probably already half-doing.** If you
  have scattered AI policies, ad hoc risk reviews, and informal model
  documentation, ISO 42001's clause structure gives you a place to put all
  of it in an auditable form.
- **Don't confuse certification with legal compliance.** It doesn't satisfy
  EU AI Act high-risk obligations on its own — treat it as complementary,
  not a shortcut.
- **Start with the Statement of Applicability exercise even without
  pursuing certification.** Mapping your actual controls against the 38 in
  Annex A is a useful gap-analysis exercise regardless of whether you ever
  pay for an audit.
- **Ask AI vendors about it.** If you're procuring AI systems from
  third parties, ISO 42001 certification (or lack of it) is a reasonable
  question to add to vendor due diligence alongside SOC 2 and similar.

# 5. OECD AI Principles

*Posted 29 September 2026*

The OECD AI Principles are the reason AI regulation around the world looks
broadly similar rather than wildly divergent. Adopted in **May 2019** as the
first intergovernmental standard on AI, and **revised on 3 May 2024**, they
are non-binding — but they are the upstream source that the EU AI Act, the
G7 Hiroshima Process, the G20 AI Principles and a long list of national
strategies all draw from.

If you only learn one thing from this post: the OECD's **definition of an
"AI system"** is the definition that ended up, nearly verbatim, in the EU AI
Act. Frameworks that look unrelated often share this common ancestor.

## The five values-based principles

1. **Inclusive growth, sustainable development and well-being** — AI should
   produce beneficial outcomes for people and the planet, not just
   efficiency gains for the deployer.
2. **Human rights and democratic values, including fairness and privacy** —
   renamed and broadened in the 2024 revision. It now explicitly covers
   AI-amplified mis- and disinformation, while respecting freedom of
   expression.
3. **Transparency and explainability** — people should know when they are
   interacting with AI, understand the outputs that affect them, and be able
   to challenge them.
4. **Robustness, security and safety** — systems should function
   appropriately across their lifecycle, and risks should be traceable and
   manageable. The 2024 text sharpened this for generative-AI failure modes.
5. **Accountability** — organisations remain answerable for their AI systems,
   including through risk management applied across the lifecycle.

## The five recommendations for policymakers

Often skipped, but they explain *why* national AI strategies all rhyme:
investing in AI R&D; fostering an inclusive AI-enabling ecosystem; shaping an
enabling and **interoperable** governance and policy environment; building
human capacity and preparing for labour-market transition; and international
co-operation for trustworthy AI.

## What the 2024 revision changed

The revision was a response to general-purpose and generative AI arriving
after the 2019 text was written. The structure held — still five principles,
still five recommendations — but the language was tightened around
information integrity, privacy, intellectual property, and safety risks
specific to general-purpose systems. Notably, the update did **not** add a
new principle for generative AI; the OECD's position was that the existing
five already covered it, once the wording caught up.

## What this means in practice

- **Use it as the translation layer, not the control set.** The Principles
  won't tell you what to implement. They will tell you why the EU AI Act,
  NIST AI RMF and ISO/IEC 42001 keep asking for similar things — which makes
  it far easier to build one control set that satisfies several regimes.
- **They carry soft weight, not legal weight.** Adherence is a state-level
  political commitment, not a corporate obligation. Nobody audits you against
  the OECD Principles. But "aligned with the OECD AI Principles" is common
  language in procurement and policy documents, and it usually means "maps
  to the five values above."
- **Watch the OECD.AI Policy Observatory.** It maintains national policy
  trackers and the incidents monitor, and it is where the interoperability
  work between frameworks actually gets published.
- **Good starting point for a one-page internal AI policy.** If you need
  principles to sit at the top of an AI governance programme, these five are
  more defensible than inventing your own, and they crosswalk cleanly to
  whatever regulator turns up later.
